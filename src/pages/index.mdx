Representing orientation in 3D is useful across computing and engineering. The most intuitive approach uses **Euler angles**: three successive rotations about the coordinate axes.

> **Definition (Euler Angles):** An orientation is described by three angles:
>
> - **Yaw** $\alpha$ — rotation about the $z$-axis
> - **Pitch** $\beta$ — rotation about the $y$-axis
> - **Roll** $\gamma$ — rotation about the $x$-axis

## The Three Rotation Matrices

$$
R_z(\alpha) =
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha & 0 \\
\sin\alpha & \cos\alpha & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
R_y(\beta) =
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\beta & 0 & \sin\beta \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
-\sin\beta & 0 & \cos\beta
\end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
R_x(\gamma) =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \cos\gamma & -\sin\gamma \\
0 & \sin\gamma & \cos\gamma
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Composing these three matrices can represent **any** rotation in 3D. The set of all such rotations is the group $SO(3)$ — $3\times 3$ orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$. But rotations in 3D are **not commutative** — the order matters.

## A Common Ordering

There is no universal convention. A common choice is yaw $\to$ pitch $\to$ roll:

$$
R(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = R_z(\alpha)\, R_y(\beta)\, R_x(\gamma)
$$

<details>
  <summary>Why does the matrix product look “reversed”?</summary>

When you rotate about a newly rotated axis, conjugation appears. After applying $R_z$, rotating about the new $y$-axis is $R_z R_y R_z^{-1}$. Stacking a third rotation similarly collapses to

$$
R_z R_y R_x(u)
$$

so the matrices multiply right-to-left even though the rotations are applied left-to-right in the yaw–pitch–roll story.

</details>

## Gimbal Lock

Euler angles have a fatal flaw. Set pitch $\beta = \frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$
R\!\left(\alpha,\tfrac{\pi}{2},\gamma\right)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & -\sin(\alpha-\gamma) & \cos(\alpha-\gamma) \\
0 & \cos(\alpha-\gamma) & \sin(\alpha-\gamma) \\
-1 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Yaw $\alpha$ and roll $\gamma$ collapse into a **single** degree of freedom $\alpha - \gamma$. Motions are stuck rotating in a plane — this is **gimbal lock**. Changing $\beta$ is the only way to escape.

Reordering the axes never fixes it: every Euler-angle convention has some singular pitch (or equivalent) where two axes align and a degree of freedom is lost.

> **Takeaway:** As long as we encode 3D rotations with three Euler angles, gimbal lock is unavoidable. We need a different representation — ideally something that behaves more like a single, continuous object rather than a triple of angles.
